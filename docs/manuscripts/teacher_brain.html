TEACHER_BRAIN — Pedagogy you already know, applied to your own CS learning

Date: 2026-04-25 Use: You teach elementary school students. You know — at a deep practical level — how learning actually works in humans. You don't apply that knowledge to yourself. This file is the bridge.

The premise

Pedagogy isn't separate from CS learning. It's the substrate. The ways kids actually become competent at things — slowly, with errors, with scaffolding, with low-stakes practice, with stories — are the same ways adults do. You know this in your bones because you do it daily.

What you do FOR your students that you don't do FOR YOURSELF:

  • Provide low-stakes practice opportunities
  • Honor partial mastery without shame
  • Use stories and metaphor to anchor abstract concepts
  • Repeat the basics as the basics
  • Notice and celebrate small wins
  • Distinguish "tired" from "not capable"
  • Plan for short attention spans without judgment
  • Set clear, achievable, bounded goals per session
  • Build a routine that protects the work
  • Forgive bad days

Bring those moves home from school.

Twelve teaching principles → twelve self-applications

1. "Meet them where they are."

For kids: You don't open with the lesson plan; you open with where the kid actually is today. For yourself: Don't start a tired-mode session with the plan. Start with: "What do I actually have energy for tonight?" Then pick the riff that matches.

2. "Errors are diagnostic."

For kids: When a kid gets it wrong, you learn where their mental model breaks. For yourself: When the AI's output is wrong-feeling, that's diagnostic of your unclear thinking, not just AI failure. Ask: what assumption was wrong on my side?

3. "Repetition without shame."

For kids: A kid asks the same question for the third time. You answer it again, kindly. For yourself: You re-derive how SQLite triggers work every time you need them. That's fine. Look it up again. Adults need spaced repetition too. Don't shame yourself for the lookup.

4. "Concrete before abstract."

For kids: Counting beans before "addition." Stories before themes. For yourself: Don't read a database book then try to apply. Build something tiny first; then the chapter on B-trees lands. The book follows the experience.

5. "Scaffolding."

For kids: Worksheets with the structure pre-filled, blanks for the new skill. For yourself: Templates, briefings, riffs, prompts (this file is scaffolding). Use them. Don't pretend you should be able to start cold.

6. "Bounded sessions."

For kids: Reading time is 20 minutes, not "until done." The bound itself protects engagement. For yourself: Tired-mode sessions are 30 or 60 minutes, by timer. Bound the session before you start. Then stop.

7. "Differentiation by readiness."

For kids: The advanced reader gets harder books; the struggling one gets the same content with support. For yourself: Your projects are at different readiness. MTGSLIDER can take complex extension. DOGSGAME needs the bare-minimum prototype. Differentiate.

8. "Behavior is communication."

For kids: A kid who keeps misbehaving is telling you something. For yourself: Resistance to a project is information. Don't push through every time. Sometimes the resistance is right — the project's wrong, or the energy's wrong.

9. "Build a sense of competence."

For kids: Frequent small wins build confidence; rare big wins don't. For yourself: Lots of small completed things > rare big shipped things. The ONESCREEN_PROJECTS.md list exists for this reason. Each completed tiny thing is a competence deposit.

10. "Story is memory's hook."

For kids: A concept embedded in a story stays; a concept in a worksheet doesn't. For yourself: Your PKD/alchemy/MTG framings are exactly this principle at work. Don't apologize for it. The hermetic axioms make the database concepts stick. Just keep them as memory aids, not as architecture.

11. "Routine protects everything."

For kids: Predictable structure makes risk-taking safe. For yourself: The warm-up and cool-down routines from SKILLSRIFFING.md are exactly this. A predictable session shape lets you take riskier creative bets within it.

12. "Teachers need recovery time too."

For kids: You give yourself permission to teach badly on a hard day, and you don't beat yourself up. For yourself: Tired-mode is real. A 30-minute janitorial session on a fried Tuesday is a good day at work. Treat it that way.

What you have that most adult learners don't

A real advantage you should leverage:

  • You can spot scaffolding. Adult learners stumble through tutorials missing the obvious supports they should be using. You see scaffolding everywhere because you build it daily.
  • You're calibrated on attention. You know the difference between "kid is bored" and "kid is overloaded." Apply that calibration to yourself.
  • You know recovery routines work. Snack, bathroom, walk, then back. Apply.
  • You know when to abandon a lesson plan. When the room's energy is wrong, the plan changes. Apply this to your sessions.
  • You know that learning is uneven. Plateau, then jump, then plateau. Don't measure your CS learning week-to-week. Measure it season-to-season.

What teaching would look like if applied to your worst pattern

You said: "too tired from working with students to think straight". Here's the teacher-brain fix:

Worst pattern: tired-Ted opens an architecture-level prompt at 9pm

In a classroom analog: a teacher who's exhausted attempting a new lesson plan with no prep, when the kids are also tired. Outcome: bad lesson, demoralized teacher, kids who don't learn the thing.

What good teachers do instead at the end of a hard day: - Read-aloud time (low-energy, kids absorb language passively) → for you: read-mode, AI explaining a concept, journal entry. - Free-choice activity (kids pick from a curated set of low-stakes things) → for you: ONESCREEN_PROJECTS.md or warm-project menu. - Reflection circle (kids share one thing they noticed today) → for you: one line in LEARNING_JOURNAL.md. - Cleanup (everyone helps; no one creates) → for you: janitorial riff.

You wouldn't ask a tired class to write a research paper. Don't ask tired-yourself to design an event-bus architecture.

Three classroom moves you should steal for your sessions

1. The "do-now"

In class: A 5-minute warm-up activity that's already on the board when kids arrive. No instructions needed. For yourself: A NEXT.md or RESUME.md per project that says exactly what the do-now is. You sit down, you read it, you do it. No deciding.

2. The exit ticket

In class: Last 2 minutes. One question, written answer. "What did you learn today? What are you still confused about?" For yourself: Cool-down routine. Two minutes. Two questions. One sentence each.

3. The fortnightly reset

In class: Every two weeks, you toss what isn't working and try something new for one period. For yourself: Every two weeks, look at your warm-project default. Is it still working? If a different project would be the warm one for the next fortnight, rotate it.

Small homework (your idea, for yourself)

Pick ONE student you're teaching this year. Imagine they were the one trying to learn CS like you are. What would you do for them?

Now: do that for yourself.

You'd probably: - Find them low-stakes practice - Give them small, achievable goals - Forgive their bad days - Celebrate their tiny wins - Tell them their hermetic metaphors are good memory aids - Make them stop at the timer - Read aloud sometimes instead of asking them to write - Notice when they're tired and adjust

That's the protocol. Apply it.
